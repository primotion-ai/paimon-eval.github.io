01 / THE IDEA

Progress is more than
time passing.

A robot can keep moving without moving closer to its goal. Understanding the difference is essential.

Progress models measure how far a robot has advanced toward completing a task. They can provide rewards for reinforcement learning, reveal failures, and help make better use of robot data. Yet most existing models derive progress labels from elapsed trajectory time, which often fails to reflect actual task progress and leads to inaccurate predictions. They also lack mechanisms explicitly designed for generalization, relying instead on extensive vision-language training. The generalization this provides is often unreliable, so adapting to new tasks typically still requires fine-tuning.

We introduce Yuheng, an in-context progress model that takes a few annotated keyframes from one successful trajectory as its reference. Those keyframes define what progress means—so the model can evaluate new trajectories against the same semantic scale. Yuheng works across grippers, dexterous hands, and human hands, and can use a reference from one embodiment to evaluate another performing the same task.

Yuheng · 玉衡
Named after Yuheng, one of the seven stars of the Big Dipper. Its name evokes balance and measurement, guiding how we measure robot progress.

03 / CAPABILITIES IN ACTION

Four capabilities.
A clearer picture of progress.

From choosing the progress scale to transferring it across tasks and embodiments.

3.1 / USER-DEFINED PROGRESS SCALES

Your milestones. Your measure of progress.

Users define the progress scale by assigning values to keyframes from a successful reference trajectory. The same milestone might mean 20% progress to one user and 60% to another; without an explicit reference, these differences make progress ambiguous and lead to inconsistencies in annotation and downstream use.

Through in-context learning, Yuheng uses these labeled keyframes to make the intended scale explicit. The reference resolves ambiguity about what each progress value means, providing a shared basis for evaluating other trajectories of the same task.

3.2 / SEMANTIC PROGRESS ALIGNMENT

Different trajectories. Matching semantic milestones.

Frames at the same semantic stage receive similar progress values on the reference scale, even when trajectories differ in execution speed, motion, or outcome.

This alignment makes progress comparable across successful, suboptimal, and failed executions, and helps retrieve corresponding keyframes from different trajectories.

3.3 / CROSS-EMBODIMENT TRANSFER

Different embodiments. A shared measure of progress.

Label keyframes from one successful trajectory to define a progress scale. Yuheng uses this reference to evaluate the same task across human hands, grippers, and dexterous hands.

3.4 / ONE-SHOT MULTI-TASK GENERALIZATION

A new task. One successful reference.

For each new task, provide its instruction and one successful reference trajectory. Yuheng uses that context to evaluate progress on unseen tasks and objects without task-specific fine-tuning.

It reasons about the relationship between the reference keyframes and the current frame, allowing the same model to work across different tasks.

04 / A BETTER MEASURE

Better labels.
Fairer comparisons.

A progress model should be judged throughout a task—not only at its endpoint. We introduce a benchmark with annotations generated by GPT and then corrected by human annotators to evaluate progress across the full course of execution.

Designed to improve on the progress annotations used in Robometer and RoboReward, it provides a fairer basis for comparing what different progress models actually understand.

What each metric measures

Within-trajectory agreement

For fixed pairs of frames from one trajectory, we compare the model’s progress ordering (<, ≈, >) with the benchmark labels. A difference of at most 0.1 is a tie. The score is the percentage of correctly matched relations.

Cross-trajectory agreement

The same relation test is applied to pairs of frames from different trajectories, checking whether progress is comparable across executions. Results cover S ↔ S, N ↔ N, and S ↔ N pairs.

Terminal outcome agreement

The last-frame progress predicts failure [0, 0.33), suboptimal execution [0.33, 0.66), or success [0.66, 1]. The score is the percentage of trajectories whose predicted outcome matches the benchmark annotation.

Mean absolute error

MAE = 100 × mean |model progress − benchmark progress| over all frames, with equal weight per frame. It measures absolute calibration in percentage points; lower is better. No tie tolerance is applied.
